# What Is a Safe PTV? Understanding Pendulum Test Values

When a floor is slip-tested, the result comes back as a **Pendulum Test Value**, or PTV. It's a single number — but people often misunderstand what it means, and that misunderstanding can lead to either false confidence or unnecessary panic. This guide explains what a "safe" PTV actually is, and the nuances the specialists want you to know.

## The three slip-potential bands

The UK Slip Resistance Group (UKSRG) and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) classify floors into three bands based on their PTV:

| Slip potential | Pendulum Test Value (PTV) |
| --- | --- |
| High | 0 – 24 |
| Moderate | 25 – 35 |
| Low | 36 and above |

So the short answer to "what's a safe floor?" is: one that achieves a **PTV of 36 or above** in the conditions in which it's actually used.

## Where the "36" comes from

The threshold isn't arbitrary. It traces back to research carried out by the **Building Research Station in the late 1950s**, which measured the coefficient of friction that able-bodied people need to walk safely on the level. That work produced a scale of slip risk:

| Coefficient of friction | Risk of slipping |
| --- | --- |
| 0.19 | 1 in 2 |
| 0.24 | 1 in 20 |
| 0.29 | 1 in 1,000 |
| 0.34 | 1 in 100,000 |
| 0.36 | 1 in 1,000,000 |

Because a PTV is roughly 100 times the coefficient of friction, a coefficient of 0.36 — a one-in-a-million slip risk — corresponds to a PTV of 36. That is why 36 became the benchmark for "low slip potential."

## Wet or dry — which figure matters?

This is where many people go wrong. A floor might score a comfortable PTV in the dry and a dangerous one in the wet. Since most slips happen on contaminated surfaces, the **wet result is usually the critical figure** for any area that can get wet — entrances in bad weather, kitchens, bathrooms, pool surrounds, and so on.

That said, UKSRG cautions against treating "wet PTV 36" as a rigid, universal minimum. Millions of people safely cross floors every day whose wet PTV is below 36 — because those floors stay dry in normal use and any spills are cleaned up quickly. The right standard depends on how the space is genuinely used.

## PTV is not a simple pass/fail

The guidelines are explicit: a PTV should be interpreted as part of a **risk assessment**, not as a pass/fail line. Several factors matter alongside the number:

- The test itself has an inherent uncertainty of one to two units, so a PTV of 35 is not meaningfully more dangerous than a 36.
- Footwear, contamination and cleaning regimes all affect real-world risk.
- The floor manufacturer's guidance on suitable end-uses should be taken into account.

In other words, the PTV tells you a great deal — but it is evidence to be weighed, not a verdict on its own.

## Barefoot areas are tested differently

For areas used barefoot — swimming pools, changing rooms, spa and bathroom floors — the test uses a softer **Slider 55 (TRRL)** rubber rather than the **Slider 96 (4S)** used for shod areas. A gym changing room used both ways may be tested with both.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a higher PTV always better?**
Generally yes for slip safety, but very high-grip surfaces can be harder to clean or uncomfortable underfoot, so the aim is a surface that is appropriately safe for its use, not simply the highest possible number.

**My floor scored 35. Is it dangerous?**
Not necessarily. A 35 sits at the top of the "moderate" band and, given test uncertainty, is close to the low-risk threshold. A specialist can advise whether treatment is warranted based on how the area is used.

**Can an anti-slip treatment raise my PTV?**
Yes — a properly applied treatment can lift an existing floor into the low-slip band, and a re-test documents the result.
